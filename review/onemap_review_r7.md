Yes — reads as real buildings painted pastel.

The cloud-shaped roofs and pastel palette are doing the "toy" work, but the facades underneath are still realistic street architecture: flat rectangular wall planes, standard-height doors/windows in a grid, and shopfront proportions lifted straight from a real Taiwanese streetscape. Swap the roofs back to tile and this reads as a normal (if colorful) street.

Top 5 issues:
1. Facades are flat vertical planes with real-building proportions (window-to-wall ratio, door height, multi-story scale) — no chunky foreshortening, bulging walls, or oversized/undersized toy proportions.
2. 鐵窗花 grilles render as plain flat rings/portholes, not the layered, pierced ironwork lattice the cultural detail calls for — they read as window trim, not grillework, losing the intended detail.
3. Materials are uniform flat-toon shading with no differentiated toy material cues (no felt/plastic/wood-block texture breaks, stitching, or seams) — everything shares one glossy plastic finish so nothing pops as a distinct "piece."
4. Signage/typography (書局, 柑仔店, 冰店) is rendered as realistic printed signboards rather than chunky dimensional toy lettering or iconography.
5. Asset fidelity mismatch — the dog is a blocky, visibly low-poly model next to smoothly shaded rounded environment props, breaking the cohesive toy-diorama read.

Top 3 strengths:
1. Rooftop silhouettes (bubble/cloud canopies) are the strongest and most successful "toy" shape language in the scene.
2. Bedroom interior reads well — rounded bed, soft toy chest, bear plush, round rug sell a believable kids'-toy room.
3. Color harmony (lavender/peach/mint pastel palette) is consistent and appealing across street and interior.
